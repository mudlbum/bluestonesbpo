    a: "Within three months of the end of the fiscal year, with an interim return during the year. For a company on a December year end that means an end-of-March deadline.   - q: "Do we have to withhold tax on payments to our parent company?"
    a: "Generally yes. Payments from a Korean entity to a non-resident — dividends, interest, royalties, and many service fees — are subject to Korean withholding tax at domestic rates, which a tax treaty may reduce where one applies and where the correct documentation is in place before payment. The order matters: treaty relief is claimed with supporting documentation, not assumed retrospectively. We see real money lost here, by companies who withheld at the full domestic rate for years while a treaty rate was available."
  - q: "What is a permanent establishment risk and why does it matter?"
    a: "If your overseas company has activity in Korea that goes beyond preparatory or auxiliary work — people concluding contracts, a fixed place of business carrying on the core activity — Korea may treat it as a permanent establishment and tax the profits attributable to it, whether or not you registered anything. This most often bites companies operating through a liaison office that quietly started selling, or through employees working in Korea for a foreign parent. ## The Korean filing calendar is dense

Korea does not have a light-touch compliance calendar. A typical foreign-owned corporation deals
with VAT four times a year, monthly withholding filings, an interim corporate tax return, an
annual corporate tax return, the payroll year-end settlement, and — if it transacts with its
parent — transfer pricing disclosure alongside the annual return.

None of these are individually difficult. The failure mode is not complexity; it is that a small
Korean office with one finance person and a part-time bookkeeper simply runs out of calendar.

> [!KEY] Missing a deadline is a cash cost, not a paperwork cost
> Korean penalties for late filing and late payment are formulaic and accrue automatically.
> There is no goodwill discount for a foreign company that did not realise.

## VAT — the rhythm of the year

Korean VAT runs at a standard rate of 10 percent, with zero-rating for exports and exemption for
certain categories of supply. The taxable year is split into two periods, each carrying a
preliminary and a final return, which is why a corporation files quarterly.

Three things matter more than the mechanics:

- **Tax invoices are the evidence.** Korea runs an electronic tax invoice system, and input VAT
  recovery depends on invoices being issued and transmitted correctly and on time. A supplier
  who issues late, or to the wrong registration number, creates a problem in *your* return.
- **Exports need substantiation.** Zero-rating is a claim you have to support with the shipping
  and customs evidence, not a status you assert.
- **Import VAT and customs interact.** If you bring goods in, the customs valuation and the VAT
  recovery position are connected, and FTA origin claims sit alongside both.

> [!TIP] Reconcile VAT to revenue every quarter, not every year
> The cheapest tax review available is checking that VAT-reported supply ties to booked revenue
> each quarter. Where it does not, you have found either a filing error or a bookkeeping error
> while it is still small.

## Corporate income tax

The annual return is due within three months of the fiscal year end, with an interim return
during the year. Because it is built on the finalised statutory accounts, the tax deadline is
effectively a deadline on your year-end close.

For a foreign-owned subsidiary, the areas that most often need attention are:

| Area | Why it needs care |
| --- | --- |
| Deductibility of head-office charges | Management fees and cost recharges must be supported and arm's length, or they are disallowed |
| Entertainment expenses | Korea limits and formalises deduction in ways foreign parents rarely expect |
| Interest on related-party loans | Thin capitalisation and arm's-length interest rules both apply |
| Provisions and accruals | Book treatment and tax treatment diverge; the difference has to be tracked |
| Tax losses | Carry-forward is available but time-limited and conditional |

## Cross-border payments and treaty relief

This is where we most often find recoverable money.

A Korean entity paying dividends, interest, royalties or certain service fees to a non-resident
withholds Korean tax. Korea has an extensive treaty network, and many of those treaties reduce
the applicable rate — but relief depends on the recipient's residence being established and the
documentation being in place **before** the payment is made.

> [!WARNING] Treaty relief is documented in advance, not claimed afterwards
> Companies that withhold at the full domestic rate for two years and then discover a treaty
> rate applied face a refund process, not a simple correction — and refund windows are finite.

## Transfer pricing without over-engineering it

Every foreign-owned subsidiary that recharges costs, pays a royalty, buys from its parent or
provides services to the group has a transfer pricing position. The question is only whether it
has been documented.

The proportionate approach for a small entity is not a full global master file. It is:

> [!ACTION] Minimum viable transfer pricing file
> - A written description of each related-party transaction and why it exists
> - The pricing method chosen, and why it fits that transaction
> - Evidence the price is within an arm's-length range
> - Intercompany agreements that actually match what happens in practice
> - Annual disclosure filed with the corporate return where required

The last point is the one that fails most often: agreements drafted years ago describing a
relationship that has since changed. An auditor reads the agreement, then the ledger, and asks
why they differ.
