faq:
  - q: "Does a Korean liaison office have to file anything with the tax office?"
    a: "Yes, three things, even though it files no corporate income tax return. The main one is the liaison office status report under Article 94-2 of the Corporate Tax Act, due to the district tax office having jurisdiction by 10 February of the year following the year it covers. Where the office is issued tax invoices, it submits a sum of tax invoices by place of purchase for each half year, by 25 July and 25 January. Where it is issued ordinary invoices, it submits a sum of those by 10 February as well. If the office employs anyone in Korea, monthly or semi-annual withholding returns sit on top of all three."
  - q: "What is the penalty for not filing the Korean liaison office status report?"
    a: "An administrative fine of KRW 5 million, set by Table 2 of the Enforcement Decree of the Corporate Tax Act under Article 124 of the Act itself. It is not automatic on the missed deadline. The sequence is that the office fails to submit the report or submits false data, the head of the district tax office issues a correction order, and the fine attaches when that order is not complied with. The statutory ceiling is KRW 10 million and the Decree sets the applied amount at KRW 5 million. It bites on non-submission or false submission occurring on or after 1 January 2026."
  - q: "When does a liaison office become a taxable place of business in Korea?"
    a: "When it stops doing only preparatory and auxiliary work. A liaison office is defined by what it is allowed to do — business liaison, market research, information gathering, advertising for the overseas head office. A domestic place of business is a fixed place where essential and important business activities are carried out, and it also arises where the foreign company operates in Korea through a person with authority to conclude contracts on its behalf. Cross that line and the office is a permanent establishment: it owes Korean corporate income tax on the income attributable to it, and the exposure runs backwards, not from the date someone notices."
  - q: "Does a liaison office withhold Korean payroll tax on its staff?"
    a: "It depends on who pays them, and this is the point most groups get wrong. Where the liaison office itself pays a Korean-employed member of staff, ordinary Korean wage withholding applies and the office files withholding returns like any other employer. Where the employee is paid directly by the overseas head office, there is no Korean withholding agent, and the National Tax Service's own guidance sets out two routes: the employee joins a taxpayers' association for wage income received from a non-resident, or files an annual return themselves. Neither route is optional, and neither happens by default."
  - q: "Can a liaison office invoice customers or earn revenue in Korea?"
    a: "No. It cannot carry on profit-making business in Korea, which is why it receives a unique identification number from the tax office rather than a business registration number and files no corporate tax return. The status report exists precisely to test that. It asks for the head office's domestic transaction position, other Korean branches, Korean customers and contract agents — the facts that would show the office is in reality selling, contracting or delivering services, which is what turns it into a taxable place of business."

## The report was already mandatory. Now it has a price

If your group runs a representative office in Korea, someone has to file a liaison office status report with the district tax office by 10 February each year. That has been law since the 2022 tax year. What changed this year is that ignoring it now costs KRW 5 million, and the first filing under the new regime falls due on 10 February 2027.

The fine is not triggered by the missed deadline on its own. Article 124 of the Corporate Tax Act, as amended by the reform that passed in December 2025, works in two steps: the foreign corporation fails to submit the status data or submits false data, the head of the district tax office issues a correction order, and the fine attaches when that order is not complied with. Table 2 of the Enforcement Decree sets the amount at KRW 5 million against a statutory ceiling of KRW 10 million. The amended Decree was promulgated on 27 February 2026 and applies to failures occurring from 1 January 2026 onward.

> [!KEY] **KRW 5 million**, under Article 124 of the Corporate Tax Act and Table 2 of its Enforcement Decree, for failing to submit or falsely submitting liaison office status data and then breaching the tax office's correction order. Applies from **1 January 2026**.

Two steps sounds like a safety net. In practice it is a trap of a different shape, because the correction order goes to the foreign corporation, and the foreign corporation is the overseas head office. A letter in Korean, addressed to a parent company that does not have a Korean finance function, arriving at an office whose entire staff is two people doing market research, is not a letter that reliably gets read.

## Fifty-six per cent of these offices have never filed

The reason the government attached a penalty is not subtle. National Tax Service data given to the National Assembly in October 2025 showed that of 3,304 liaison offices required to file the status report over three years, 1,867 did not. The submission rate was 40.7% in 2022, 45.2% in 2023 and 44.5% in 2024 — never half.

The concentration is worth knowing. US-owned offices accounted for 537 of the violations and Chinese-owned offices 296, together 44.7% of the total. Both nationalities exceeded the average non-filing rate, at 66.6% and 70.8% respectively.

> [!WARNING] A very large majority of liaison offices in Korea have never filed this report, so the fact that nobody at your group has mentioned it is not evidence that it does not apply. Assume it does, and check the last three years.

That last point matters for anyone reading this from a head office. The compliance base rate here is so low that the usual reassurance — *our Korean advisers would have told us* — carries almost no weight. Many liaison offices were opened by an HR or facilities team, not a finance one, and have never had a Korean tax adviser attached to them at all.

## What the status report actually asks

The 현황명세서 is not a return. It reports no income and calculates no tax. It is an evidence form, and understanding what it is testing tells you why the tax office cares.

It asks for the basic facts about the office, the current status of the overseas head office, any other Korean branches of the same foreign corporation, and — the part that matters — the head office's Korean transaction position, its Korean customers and its contracting agents in Korea.

Those are the facts that would show the office is doing something other than liaison work. A representative office with a customer list, a Korean contract agent and a stream of head office invoices going to Korean buyers is describing a sales operation. The form is the National Tax Service asking every liaison office to state, in writing and annually, the facts on which its own non-taxable status depends.

Which is also why a false submission carries the same penalty as no submission. The regime was introduced in the 2021 tax reform for a stated reason: foreign corporations setting up a liaison office instead of constituting a permanent establishment, and then using it as one.

## The line between liaison and taxable, and what crossing it costs

A liaison office may carry out preparatory or supporting activities for its overseas head office — business liaison, market research, information gathering, advertising. It may not conduct profit-making business in Korea. That is why it holds a unique identification number from the tax office rather than a business registration number, and why it files no corporate income tax return.

A domestic place of business, by contrast, is a fixed place where essential and important business activities are carried out. It also arises where a foreign corporation operates in Korea through a person who has authority to conclude contracts on its behalf, which is the version that catches a "liaison" office whose head negotiates and signs.

| | Liaison office | Domestic place of business |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Permitted activity | Preparatory and supporting only | Essential and important business activity |
| Tax office identifier | Unique identification number | Business registration number |
| Corporate income tax return | No | Yes |
| VAT return | No | Yes, four times a year |
| Payroll withholding | Yes, where it pays Korean staff | Yes |
| Annual status report | Yes, by 10 February | No |

The consequence of being on the wrong side of that line is not the KRW 5 million. It is corporate income tax on the income attributable to the Korean operation, assessed retrospectively, with penalty tax on top, plus the VAT that should have been charged and the withholding that should have been operated. The fine is the cheapest item on the list, which is precisely why the government treated it as a way to force disclosure rather than as a punishment in itself.

> [!NOTE] The NTS manual on liaison offices sets out worked cases where an office was held to constitute a domestic place of business. If your Korean office signs anything, negotiates price, or delivers services to Korean group companies, read those before you conclude you are outside.

## The rest of the calendar

The status report is not the only thing a liaison office files, and the other items are easier to miss because they are conditional.

Where the office is issued tax invoices — which it will be, for rent, utilities and professional fees — it submits a sum of tax invoices by place of purchase for each half year: 1 January to 30 June is filed between 1 and 25 July, and the second half between 1 and 25 January. Where it is issued ordinary invoices, the sum of those is due by 10 February, for goods or services supplied on or after 1 January 2023.

If the office employs anyone in Korea and pays them, it is a withholding agent and files withholding returns on the same schedule as any other employer. Where staff are paid directly by the overseas head office instead, there is no Korean withholding agent at all, and the NTS guidance sets out the two routes that then apply: the employee joins a taxpayers' association for wage income received from a non-resident, or files an annual return personally. This is one of the most commonly unmanaged exposures in a Korean representative office, because from the group's point of view nothing about the payroll is Korean.

> [!DEADLINE] Liaison office status report for the **2026** year: due **10 February 2027**, and the first one carrying the new fine. Sum of invoices by place of purchase: also **10 February**. Sum of tax invoices: **25 July** and **25 January**.

## What to check before February

The useful exercise here is not the form. It is the three years behind it.

> [!ACTION] Confirm whether a status report was filed for 2023, 2024 and 2025, and by whom. Read the last twelve months of the Korean office's activity against the preparatory-and-auxiliary test, paying particular attention to who signs, who negotiates and who invoices. Check whether staff are paid by the Korean office or by the head office, and if the latter, what income tax route they are on. Then diarise 10 February 2027 with a named owner, in Korea.

If the answer to the first question is that nobody filed, the position is recoverable and much better addressed voluntarily than after a correction order lands at a head office that cannot read it. If the answer to the second is that the office has been selling, the liaison structure is the wrong one and has probably been the wrong one for some time — at which point the question becomes whether to convert to a branch or a [foreign-invested subsidiary](/blog/entity/korea-fdi-threshold-foreign-invested-company/), and what the historical exposure looks like.

Neither of those is a February job. The February job is the form, and the form is now the cheap part.
